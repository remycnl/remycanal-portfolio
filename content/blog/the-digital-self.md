Every item I save, post or add to a wishlist is a small piece of a story about who I am, or who I want to be. This is a look at how I build and curate an identity through what I consume online.

## My feed, seen like a stranger

Scrolling Instagram the other day, I caught myself analyzing my own feed like a stranger. That minimalist coffee table I posted last month (obviously). The sustainable sneakers still sitting in my cart. That vintage record player positioned to show I'm “into analog stuff.”

Here's the thing: each item isn't just an item. They're pieces of a narrative I'm constructing about who I am. Or maybe who I want to be?

## A self that extends online

Social media has completely changed identity-through-shopping. Now there's this weird gap between what I actually own (sitting in my messy apartment) and what I present online. Multiple versions of me exist: the one who buys practical Amazon stuff, and the one who curates aesthetic Pinterest wishlists.

Belk's Extended Self theory says our possessions become part of our identity. But online? It's way more complex. My digital self isn't just what I own, it's what I browse, like and save to wishlists. My Pinterest folder full of artisanal mugs and Japanese stationery says more about my identity than my actual apartment, because digital collections represent aspiration without budget constraints.

## Curating a public self

What unsettles me is how consciously I curate this. When I create a board titled “My Aesthetic” or consider whether to post something, I'm not passively consuming. I'm actively constructing a public self through consumption signals.

The pressure feels constant. “Should I post this? Does it align with my minimalist aesthetic?” The anxiety isn't about the object, it's about what it communicates. My consumption choices have become performances, and I'm both actor and audience, judging the authenticity.

## Four versions of me

Okay, trying to make sense of all this. The more I think about it, the weirder it gets.

I'm maintaining multiple versions of myself:

- What I actually own (laptop, IKEA furniture)
- What I wish I owned (Pinterest fantasy)
- What I show (Edwige, my over-photographed plant)
- What algorithms think I am (creepy, because I don't even know that version)

These don't match up. My actual shopping habits versus my Pinterest saves? Completely different people. That gap feels uncomfortable, like I'm lying to myself.

## Is this on-brand?

Why do this? Goffman said we're all performing. Online, that performance is on steroids. When I post my plant-based meal kit, I'm saying “look, I care about the environment.” Even unconsciously.

Can we talk about “personal branding”? I catch myself thinking “is this on-brand?” before buying. When did I become a brand? Last week I almost skipped comfortable shoes because they didn't fit my aesthetic. That's messed up.

The nice part? I can explore identities without committing. My minimalist Pinterest exists while my apartment is a mess. But isn't that fake? Saving eco-friendly brands makes me feel ethical without changing anything. Pretending with extra steps.

Bourdieu talked about consumption as class markers. Honestly? Not my experience. When I choose vintage stuff, I genuinely just like it. Not every decision is social positioning.

## A loop that narrows who I can be

Platforms are designed for this. Pinterest is technically public, a weird duality. Sometimes I'm genuinely exploring. Other times I'm curating for an imaginary audience. Even when I'm not performing, the possibility that someone sees it shapes my choices.

Algorithmic curation is trippy. The more I engage, the more it shows me that stuff, which makes me engage more. It's a loop narrowing who I can be.

Here's what messes with my head: I genuinely want those sustainable sneakers. But once they're on a public wishlist, something shifts. Do I actually want this, or have I created curated desires separate from my real preferences? I don't trust my own wants anymore. Am I performing for others or for myself?

## Not all bad

Looking back, I'm torn. Part of me thinks this is creative. Another part is like, this is deeply messed up.

It's not all bad. I've used Pinterest for room designs before buying anything. Trying different versions without spending money is valuable. I've made real friends through sustainable fashion communities: it started as performing but ended with actual friendships. Making Pinterest boards takes real thought, it's creative expression.

## An impossible standard

But the bad parts are harder to ignore. The pressure to match my “aesthetic” is exhausting. Thinking “would I be okay showing this?” before buying isn't normal. If I buy something “wrong,” it feels bad, like I've messed up my identity. Over a purchase. Ridiculous.

Here's what I don't love admitting: my Instagram saves are full of sustainable brands. Organic cotton, fair trade. Meanwhile, my actual closet? Mostly Zalando and ASOS, what I can afford on a student budget. Instead of motivating me, it makes me feel shitty. Like I'm failing at being the person I present. My aspirational self became an impossible standard.

When identity is tied to what I buy, not affording something feels like personal failure. Not “oh well,” but “I'm failing at being myself.” Worth gets tangled with buying ability. Really unhealthy.

## Who benefits

Every save or browse becomes data to profile me and sell me stuff. My identity is literally turned into a product sold to advertisers. Kind of dystopian.

Who benefits? I get some stuff (community, creativity). Mostly? Platforms and advertisers. They profit from my content, my data, my browsing time. I'm doing identity work, and the value goes to companies.

## Not deleting my apps

After thinking through all this, I'm not deleting my apps. Not realistic. But I want more awareness.

### Paying attention

Pause before posting or saving and ask “do I want this or am I performing?” Create space to think. Every few months, audit my wishlists and ask if they reflect me. Notice how platforms manipulate, when I'm nudged for their benefit.

### Buying without performance

Use incognito more when browsing. Spend more time on actual hobbies and real conversations. Stop trying to match everything to my aesthetic. It's okay to like random things. Identity doesn't need perfect curation.

### Smaller platforms, private spaces

Maybe try smaller platforms not designed to extract value. Go back to physical mood boards sometimes, actually private. Spaces like r/Anticonsumption, where people question instead of celebrate shopping.

## What I'm doing

- Noticing when I'm performing versus wanting.
- Having areas where I just buy needs.
- Checking whether my digital identity helps me or runs my life.
- Being less hard on myself about my aspirational identity: the gap is built in, not a personal failure.

Writing this has been uncomfortable. It made me see how much “being myself” is careful performance, and how tangled identity and shopping are. But awareness is the first step. I'm not deleting Instagram or Pinterest, not realistic. Maybe I can be more conscious about when I'm performing versus just being.

The digital me is still me, in a way. But I want more control. Less of what algorithms want, more of what I actually want.

> My identity shouldn't be handed over to platforms selling stuff.

I'll probably still waste time curating my aesthetic though. Baby steps.
