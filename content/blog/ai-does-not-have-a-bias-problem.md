When an AI system discriminates, we call it a bug and ask engineers to patch it. But the model is doing exactly what it was built to do: find patterns in what we gave it. The uncomfortable question isn't what's wrong with the machine, it's what the machine just learned about us.

## A mirror that takes notes

In 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon had scrapped an experimental recruiting tool. The system had been trained on ten years of résumés submitted to the company, most of them from men, a reflection of male dominance across the tech industry. It learned the pattern. It started downgrading résumés containing the word “women's,” as in “women's chess club captain,” and marked down graduates of two all-women's colleges.

Nobody wrote a sexist rule. No engineer typed anything like `if woman: lower_score`. The model simply found the most reliable signal in the history it was handed, and that history said hiring looks like this.

That's the part everyone knows. The more interesting bias is the one that gets in before any data does.

## The bias is in the question

Sometimes the data is perfectly accurate and the problem is what we asked for. In 2019, researchers published a study in *Science* about an algorithm used widely across the US health system to decide which patients should get extra care. It predicted future healthcare costs, using cost as a stand-in for need.

That sounds reasonable until you remember that less money is spent on Black patients than on white patients with the same level of illness, because of unequal access and unequal treatment. So at the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker. Changing what the model was asked to predict shrank the gap substantially.

The model was good at predicting cost. Cost just wasn't what anyone cared about. There's no villain in this story, only someone picking a convenient column in a database.

## Who counts as “normal”

In 2018, Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial systems that classify gender from face photos. The error rate was 0.8% for lighter-skinned men and up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women. The benchmark datasets the industry used were overwhelmingly lighter-skinned and male, so the benchmark itself had quietly defined what “normal” looks like.

The hopeful part is what happened next. Once the gap had a number attached to it, it got fixed: follow-up audits found that the targeted systems had improved. Measuring was the intervention.

## There is no neutral setting

In 2016, ProPublica reported that COMPAS, a risk tool used in US courts, wrongly flagged Black defendants as higher risk almost twice as often as white defendants who didn't go on to reoffend. The company's answer was that its scores were equally accurate for both groups: a given score meant the same likelihood of reoffending.

Both claims were true. They were just measuring fairness in different ways, and researchers later showed why they can't both be satisfied: when two groups have different base rates, you can't equalize every kind of fairness at once, unless the predictor is perfect.

| Definition | In plain words | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demographic parity | The model flags the same share of each group | Ignores real differences in what's being predicted |
| Equal error rates | People who don't reoffend are wrongly flagged at the same rate in every group | A given score can then mean different things for different groups |
| Calibration | A score of 7 means the same likelihood of the outcome, whoever you are | Error rates can then differ between groups |

Choosing between these isn't a technical question. It's a question about which mistakes we're willing to tolerate, and who pays for them. No amount of extra data answers it.

> A model doesn't invent our values. It inherits them, then applies them a million times a day.

## Mirrors have feedback loops

Models aren't passive mirrors either. Predictive policing sends officers where crime was recorded, which produces more records there, which sends more officers. Recommendation systems learn what we click, then shape what we see, then what we click. Generative models can exaggerate: when Bloomberg analyzed thousands of images generated by Stable Diffusion in 2023, high-paying jobs skewed toward lighter skin tones and men, more strongly than the real-world numbers do.

A mirror that feeds back into what it reflects is no longer a mirror. It becomes part of the system.

## What I do with this

I don't train models, but I build the screens where their output turns into a decision: a score, a ranking, a “recommended for you.” That's where design can either hide the judgment or put it in front of a human. Before I ship anything that leans on a model, these are the questions I want answered:

- What is the label really measuring, and is it what we actually care about?
- Who is missing from the data, and who is missing from the room?
- What does a wrong answer cost, and who pays for it?
- Can a person see why the system decided something, contest it and reach a human?
- How will we know it's drifting once it's live, and for which groups?

None of these needs a PhD. All of them need someone to ask.

Calling it “AI bias” lets us treat the problem as a product defect, something to patch in the next release. It's closer to an audit: the model shows us our history, our shortcuts and our priorities, at a speed and scale that make them impossible to ignore. That's uncomfortable. It's also good news. A pattern we put there is a pattern we can change.
